The Problem

Pump Not Running When The Water Rises?

A sump pump does nothing for months and then has to work perfectly in the middle of a downpour. When it fails, water backs up in the pit and spreads across a garage floor, a stairwell, or a finished lower level.

7-10 yrs
Typical working life of a sump pump
Signs You Need This

When To Call About Your Sump Pump

  • 1
    It runs constantly or cycles every few seconds, which usually points to a stuck float or a failed check valve.
  • 2
    It never turns on even when the pit is full of water.
  • 3
    It grinds, rattles, or hums without moving water.
  • 4
    Water stays high in the pit after the pump finishes a cycle.
  • 5
    The discharge outside is dry or weak while the pump runs, which can mean a blocked or crushed line.

Sump Pumps In A Slab-On-Grade Town

Most Queen Creek homes sit on a slab with no basement, so a sump pump is the exception here and not the rule. Where we do find one, it is usually a pit at the low point of a garage floor or stairwell, a basement in a custom or large-lot home, a landscape basin tied to yard drains, or the collection end of a perimeter drain.

Caliche, the hard cemented soil layer under much of the East Valley, absorbs water slowly. Once a pit fills, there are few places for the water to go, which makes a working pump matter more than the number of pumps in town would suggest.

Why Monsoon Season Is The Real Test

The monsoon officially runs from June 15 through September 30, according to the National Weather Service, and a single storm can drop a lot of water in minutes. A pump that idled for nine months has to start on the first try, with a clear discharge line and a working float.

Storms also take down power. If your pump has no battery backup, it stops at the exact moment the pit fills fastest. For a pit that protects finished space or a garage, that backup is worth a real conversation.

Want it checked before the next storm? Call (480) 741-9332.

How We Diagnose A Failing Pump

We start by filling the pit with water and watching a full cycle. That shows whether the float rises, whether the pump starts, and whether it empties the pit or short-cycles.

Next we check the check valve for backflow when the pump stops, trace the discharge line to where it exits the property, and confirm the power supply and any battery backup. Then we tell you plainly whether it is a repair or a replacement.

Repair Or Replace

A stuck float, a worn check valve, or a clogged discharge line is usually a repair. A pump with a corroded housing, a failing motor, or more than eight or ten years behind it is usually better replaced, sized to the pit it serves.

A pump only moves the water that reaches the pit. If a pit keeps flooding despite a healthy pump, the cause may be grading or drainage upstream, and we will say so honestly instead of selling you a bigger pump.
