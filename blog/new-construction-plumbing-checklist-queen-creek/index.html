New Construction Plumbing Checklist for Queen Creek, AZ

New construction still passes through a builder's own inspection process, but that's not the same as an independent check focused on your interests as the buyer. Before your final walkthrough ends, run every fixture, check under every sink and behind every toilet for leaks, test water pressure at multiple fixtures, and confirm the water heater's data plate shows the build date you expect.

What To Check Before You Move In

  • ✓
    Run every faucet and shower for several minutes, checking for leaks at every supply connection underneath.
  • ✓
    Flush every toilet and check the tank and base for any dripping or seepage.
  • ✓
    Run the dishwasher and washing machine through a cycle, checking their connections for leaks during operation.
  • ✓
    Check water pressure at multiple fixtures simultaneously, since a significant drop can indicate an undersized line or a partially closed valve.
  • ✓
    Confirm the water heater's data plate matches the home's actual build date, not a refurbished or older unit.

Why New Doesn't Mean Issue-Free

New construction plumbing goes through real inspection during the build. A municipal inspection confirms code compliance under the 2021 International Plumbing Code (IPC) Queen Creek has adopted, along with correct venting, proper pipe sizing, and appropriate materials. It does not catch every practical issue a homeowner would care about. A connection that passes inspection can still develop a slow leak once the home is actually lived in and fixtures see real daily use, which is exactly why a separate, buyer-focused check matters even on a home that's technically passed every required inspection.

Queen Creek's own housing stock skews heavily toward newer construction, the large majority of homes built after 2000, which means this checklist applies to a meaningful share of the town's actual housing rather than being a rare edge case.

Beyond The Basic Checklist

A few less obvious checks are worth adding to your walkthrough if you have time. Test the water heater's temperature and pressure relief valve by lifting the test lever briefly, confirming water discharges and then stops cleanly, since a relief valve is a real safety component, not just another fitting. Check under the kitchen sink specifically for the garbage disposal's connections, since this is one of the more commonly missed spots during a rushed builder walkthrough. And run the water softener, if the home has one, through a manual regeneration cycle to confirm it's actually functioning rather than just installed.

Documenting What You Find

Photograph anything you find, even something minor, with a timestamp if your phone supports it, and note it in writing to your builder's warranty contact rather than only mentioning it verbally during the walkthrough. A documented issue reported promptly is straightforward to resolve under warranty; an undocumented concern raised months later is a much harder conversation to have, even when the underlying problem is exactly the same.

What A Professional Pre-Move-In Visit Covers

Beyond what you can check yourself, a professional visit adds a real pressure test with a gauge rather than a rough estimate from running fixtures. It also adds a visual inspection of accessible supply lines and connections, and confirmation that the water heater's relief valve, gas or electrical connections, and venting all look correct. This is the same kind of check a home inspector might do for a resale property, applied specifically to a home that's brand new but hasn't yet had months of real daily use to reveal a connection issue on its own.

Timing It Right

The ideal window for this kind of check is after the builder's final walkthrough but before or shortly after closing, while the warranty is fully active and before you've settled in enough that a minor issue starts to feel normal rather than worth reporting. Waiting even a few months past move-in doesn't disqualify you from the warranty, but earlier is always easier, both for getting an issue fixed and for having clean documentation of when it was first noticed.

Second Homes And Investment Properties

If the new home isn't your primary residence, a rental or vacation property where issues might go unnoticed for longer stretches between visits, this kind of thorough check matters even more, not less. A slow leak that would get noticed within days in an owner-occupied home can run for weeks in a property nobody's regularly checking, turning a minor warranty-covered issue into real, avoidable water damage by the time anyone actually catches it and calls someone.

Comparing Builders Isn't The Same As Comparing Plumbing Quality

A builder's reputation for finish quality, cabinets, flooring, overall design, doesn't necessarily reflect the plumbing subcontractor's work specifically, since that's often a separate trade with its own track record entirely. Two homes from the same builder in the same community can have genuinely different plumbing outcomes depending on which crew handled that specific house. That's exactly why an independent check matters even when you trust the builder generally.

Using Your Builder's Warranty Window

Most builders offer a warranty period on plumbing systems, commonly one year, and issues caught and documented within that window are typically the builder's responsibility to fix at no cost to you. Waiting past that window to report a slow leak or fixture issue means it becomes your responsibility instead, which is exactly why an early, thorough check pays for itself even on a brand-new home.

If you find something during your own check that concerns you, getting a second, independent opinion before the warranty window closes is worth the small cost, particularly for anything involving the water heater, main shutoff valve, or any connection you can't easily inspect yourself.
